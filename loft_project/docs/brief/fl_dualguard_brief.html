Federated defense against low-rate flow-table overflow: what the new experiments show

Three new experiments today: local versus federated detectors on Mininet/Ryu, scaling the federation from 3 to 9 controllers in the simulator, and a model-family comparison (MLP, Random Forest, gradient boosting). Harm throughout means benign flows refused or evicted while active, as a % of benign flows.

Key results

  1. Federation helps on real switches, and only where it should. On Mininet/Ryu, attacks a controller never saw locally harm 13.8 % of benign flows with its local model and 0.36 % with the federated one (D1, attack A2). All 18 testbed runs fall inside or near the simulator's 10-seed range.
  2. Federated detection keeps its lead as controllers are added. With 9 controllers, F1 on attacks seen only by other controllers is 96.1 federated against 52.7 local. The same three controllers gain recall as the federation grows (73 → 89 → 87 at K = 3, 6, 9).
  3. New problem found: plain FedAvg falsely demotes more benign flows at K ≥ 6, mostly on IoT controllers (online harm 8.5–11 % against 2.6–2.9 % for pooled training). FedProx or 100 rounds recover most of the offline gap and cut online harm to about 7 %, but the remaining gap to pooled is still open.
  4. Model family matters less than federation. Random Forest leads offline by about 1 point of F1, but the MLP protects users best online (1.4 % harm against 4.8 % for Random Forest) and is 2.4 KB against 16 MB.

Nine controller domains (three variants each of enterprise, IoT and cloud traffic), nested so K = 3 ⊂ 6 ⊂ 9. Each controller trains on one attack variant (A1, A2, A3, A4, A5, A7, then repeating), so a bigger federation also pools more attack knowledge. Online harm %, mean over all controllers × 7 attacks × seeds. FedAvg always beats local, but at K = 6 and 9 it demotes about 10× more benign entries than pooled training. The worst are the IoT controllers C2, C5 and C8 (16–26 % harm), whose many low-rate periodic flows look like attack flows.

Diagnosis: partly under-training on non-IID clients, partly an open problem

Same data, K = 6 / 9, offline F1: FedAvg 30 rounds 91.5 / 89.5; 100 rounds 94.4 / 92.7; FedProx 93.1 / 92.1; 3 local epochs 90.1 / 88.6; local fine-tuning 88.0 / 86.2; pooled 95.6 / 93.8. More rounds closes most of the gap; fine-tuning hurts because it forgets other controllers' attacks.

Online, the fixes help but do not close the gap. Harm % at K = 6 / 9 (2 episode seeds): FedAvg 30 rounds 11.0 / 8.5; FedProx 7.5 / 6.9; 100 rounds 7.3 / 7.7; 3 local epochs 10.3 / 7.5; fine-tuning 10.4 / 9.3; pooled 2.6 / 2.9; local 13.5 / 11.3. On the IoT controllers every federated variant stays at 13–23 % against 4–6 % pooled. Offline every model keeps the 1 % benign budget per sampled entry, yet online FedAvg demotes 4,000–7,000 benign entries per episode against about 400–600 for pooled. A likely cause is that long-lived periodic IoT flows are scored at every poll and sit near the federated threshold. Next steps to try: calibrate on whole flows instead of single polls, and weight the federated average so the IoT clients' benign data counts more.

4 · Already established (testbed matrix, 29 Oct)

48 Mininet/Ryu runs reproduce the simulator on D1–D3 for every defense, after we found and fixed a controller timing bug (entries dated by a lagging poll clock lost their per-flow history). DualGuard v2.1 alone: 0.26–5.3 % harm against 34–45 % with no defense. Open problem: on the bigFlows trace with the mimicry attack A4, DualGuard alone harms 40.9 % (27 % simulated); with PortGuard it is 3.2 %.
